Progress report · Absolute visual localization (AVL) for GPS-denied UAVs

Finding a drone's position from its camera and a satellite map

The drone compares each camera frame with geo-tagged satellite tiles and reads its latitude and longitude from the best match. This report covers every encoder we evaluated, the datasets, the methods we added on top of the encoders, how much each one helped, and the published work each idea came from.

01Summary

The starting problem. Every encoder works well in the region it was trained on and poorly elsewhere. DenseUAV-ViT finds 96.4% of frames within 100 m on its own DenseUAV data, but only 19–33% on UAV-VisLoc. Training a new model for every region we fly is not practical.

Our strategy. Keep general, off-the-shelf encoders and help them with what the drone already knows: its heading, its altitude, its approximate position from the IMU, and several encoders that make different mistakes. Nothing is trained on the test regions.

Where we are.

  • Best single encoder: MegaLoc. With heading, domain centering and altitude scaling it averages 55.4% top-1 within 100 m over four regions (74.3 / 37.5 / 44.4 / 65.3%).
  • Best accuracy: a three-encoder ensemble (MegaLoc + Game4Loc + AnyLoc-L) reaches 85.4% top-1, and 91.7% after fusing the top-5 candidates, on region 05.
  • Biggest single lift: altitude-based scale correction, +37.5 points on region 11 (58 frames won, 4 lost, p < 10⁻¹²).
  • Searching only near the IMU-predicted position adds 12–19 points when the prior is 100 m uncertain.
  • Hardware: a Jetson Orin Nano 8 GB Super ($399 module) should run the full ensemble at about 5 fixes per second (projection, to be measured).
  • Not solved yet: metre-level accuracy (needs a learned image matcher), a fusion filter that tolerates long runs of wrong fixes, and tests with a real IMU.

02The problem and our approach

Without GPS, an inertial unit alone drifts by hundreds of metres within minutes. An absolute fix is needed: the drone looks down, and its frame is matched against a satellite map stored on board. The map is cut into square tiles, each tile is turned into a descriptor vector by an encoder once before flight, and in flight the frame's vector is compared with all tile vectors. The best tiles give the position.

The hard part is that a drone frame and a satellite tile of the same place look different: different camera, date, season, sun, rotation and scale. Encoders trained to bridge this gap learn the look of their training region.

05Methods we added

Each method is listed in the order we added it, with the paper that suggested it, what we did, and what it changed. All of them are training-free: no model weights were changed for the test regions.

5.1 A fair benchmark

Adopted

Where the idea comes from

Retrieval papers report recall of tile IDs, which cannot be compared across tile sizes. DenseUAV [7] introduced a distance-based score for the same reason. Dietterich [33] recommends McNemar's test for comparing two methods on the same test items.

  • Score = distance in metres between the top tile's centre and the GPS position. We report top-1 within 25 / 50 / 100 / 200 m, top-5, median error and the error after fusion.
  • Every run also reports chance levels: a random tile, and the nearest tile in the map. A small map makes even random guessing look accurate.
  • Differences are tested with an exact paired McNemar test, per region and pooled. With 144 frames, one frame is 0.7 points, so differences under about 5 points need the test.

5.2 Heading alignment

Adopted

Where the idea comes from

The AnyVisLoc benchmark [19] measured that a 60° heading error costs 25.7 points of localization accuracy. FoundLoc [20] kept its drone's yaw north-aligned so that its place-recognition step would work, and Kinnari et al. [23] rotate and project frames with the drone's attitude before matching. The drone knows its heading from the compass and IMU, so the encoder should not have to guess the rotation.

Satellite tiles are north-up; a drone frame can point anywhere. The old pipeline encoded each frame at four rotations and kept the best. Now the frame is turned to the 90° step nearest north using the logged heading and encoded once.

5.3 Domain centering

Adopted (flight mode)

Where the idea comes from

In high-dimensional spaces a few "hub" vectors become the nearest neighbour of almost everything. Suzuki et al. [26] showed that subtracting the data mean (centering) removes hubs. A 2025 study [27] showed that place recognition improves when the map, which is available before the flight, is used at test time. We use the map and the flight's own past frames, without any training.

All drone frames share a common component (camera colour, blur, sun), and all satellite tiles share another. Neither says anything about the place, yet both inflate similarity scores. We subtract the map's mean descriptor from the tiles and the running mean of the frames seen so far from each new frame.

5.4 Searching near the IMU prediction

Adopted

Where the idea comes from

STHN [22] localizes thermal frames within a 512 m radius of the last known position. NGPS [21] predicts the satellite search region from visual-inertial odometry velocity. FoundLoc [20] keeps a sliding window of recent positions. A drone with an IMU always has an approximate position, so it never needs to search the whole map.

We simulated a navigation prior: the true position plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ. Only tiles within 3σ of the prior are searched.

5.5 Encoder ensemble

Adopted for accuracy

Where the idea comes from

Multi-Process Fusion [24] showed that combining several place-recognition methods beats any one of them (F1 0.96 vs 0.78 for NetVLAD). Waheed et al. [25] showed that the gain comes from complementarity, methods that fail on different images, and measured it with a McNemar-style test. Our three encoders were trained on very different data (street and aerial photos, a video game, no training at all), so they should fail differently.

Each encoder's descriptor is normalized to length 1 and the three are concatenated. The cosine similarity of the long vector is then exactly the average of the three encoders' similarities: score = (cos₁ + cos₂ + cos₃) / 3. Nothing is trained, and every encoder has an equal vote.

5.6 Fusing the top-5 candidates

Adopted

Where the idea comes from

FoundLoc [20] clusters the retrieved positions with DBSCAN and keeps only the largest cluster, because a wrong match tends to be isolated while the right area collects several matches.

Five strategies are implemented (cluster, softmax mean, geometric median, weighted mean, top-1). The default cluster weights the top-5 by score, groups them within 150 m, keeps the heaviest group and averages it.

5.7 Height above ground fixes the scale

Adopted (gated)

Where the idea comes from

A 2026 study [28] estimated the drone's altitude and cropped each frame to the map's scale before retrieval, gaining 41.5 points of recall. SUES-200 [17] showed that accuracy changes with flight height, and a scale-aware method [29] recovered scale from cars in the image. Our drone does not need to estimate altitude from the image: the barometer gives it, and a public terrain model [18] turns it into height above the ground.

A frame covers k × AGL metres of ground, where k = 2·tan(field of view / 2) and AGL is the height above ground. When a frame covers twice the ground of a map tile, the encoder compares the right place at the wrong zoom.

Step 1 · Height above ground

  • AGL = logged altitude above sea level − terrain height from Copernicus GLO-30 (8 tiles, 320 MB, downloaded once).
  • r05 flies at 2,313 m above sea level but only 374 m above the ground. On r11 the terrain changes the AGL from 244 to 808 m within the evaluated frames.
  • Check: on r06's take-off pad the computed AGL is −6 to −7 m, so the flight log and the terrain model agree within a few metres.
  • In flight the terrain is read at the IMU-predicted position. Reading it 300 m away from the truth, plus a 15 m barometer bias, changed r11 by −3.5 points (not significant).

Step 2 · Camera constant k

  • UAV-VisLoc does not publish its lenses, so k was measured: a frame is compared with satellite patches of 11 sizes at its GPS position, and the best-matching size gives k.
  • One camera (3976 × 2652 px) flown over four regions at 308–778 m AGL needs one constant: k = 1.00, a 53° field of view. Two different encoders agree within 1.2%.
  • The two regions with 3000 × 2000 px frames turned out to use two different lenses (52° and 62–73°). On a real drone, k comes from the lens datasheet.

Step 3 · Crop only when needed (the gate)

The frame is cropped to the tile's ground size only when altitude says it covers more than 1.25× a tile. Smaller mismatches keep the whole frame, because its context is worth more than an exact scale.

  • On r11 the crop beats the uncropped frame in 58 frames and loses in 4 (p < 10⁻¹²). It also beats the old blind three-scale search by 9 points (p = 0.015) with a third of the encoder runs.
  • Robust to height error: ±10% error changes almost nothing; ±20% keeps more than 80% of the gain.
  • The same idea on the map side, cutting tiles at the frame's ground size, gives +29.2 points on r11 for MegaLoc and +22.2 for DenseUAV-ViT (both p < 0.001), with tile centres held fixed. This is the more encoder-independent option when the flight altitude is known before take-off.

5.8 Re-ranking with local features

Not adopted (classic features)Learned matchers next

Where the idea comes from

Most localization pipelines verify the top retrieved images with feature matching and a geometric check before trusting them. AnyVisLoc [19] compared 20 matchers for drone-to-map matching: the learned matcher RoMa reached 70.1% within 5 m against 43.9% for SIFT on aerial maps, and only 18.5% on satellite maps.

  • We tried SIFT, ORB and AKAZE with RANSAC homographies on the top-10 tiles.
  • On r10, 0 of 10 candidates were verified for 144 of 144 frames. Even the correct tile, 45 m away, produced only about 40 matches, and every fitted homography was degenerate.
  • Before we added a plausibility check, these false "verifications" dropped DenseUAV accuracy from 0.60 to 0.15. The check now protects any future matcher.
  • Conclusion: classic features cannot bridge the drone-to-satellite gap. Learned, cross-domain matchers (LightGlue [34], GIM [35], MatchAnything [36]) are the next experiment.

5.9 Visual-inertial trajectory filter

Prototype (simulated IMU)

Where the idea comes from

Every working system we found combines visual fixes with odometry. FoundLoc [20] uses an EKF on a Jetson Xavier NX; NGPS [21] uses a UKF and reaches 2.94 m RMSE at 60–150 m altitude. Our filter follows the error-state Kalman filter formulation of Solà [30].

  • 15-state error-state Kalman filter: position, velocity, attitude, accelerometer and gyro biases. Visual fixes enter as position measurements with a χ² gate that rejects outliers.
  • Tested with a simulated consumer-grade IMU on the real UAV-VisLoc flight timing (r05, AnyLoc-lite, 50 frames): median error 70 → 68 m, 95th percentile 652 → 308 m, worst case 810 → 403 m. The gate rejected 7 of the 9 fixes that were more than 200 m wrong.
  • On a simulated 5 km route the IMU alone drifted 82% of the distance flown; visual fixes alone had a 126 m median error; fused: 59 m.
  • Limitation: a single-hypothesis filter can lock itself out after a long run of wrong fixes. A particle filter or trajectory consensus, as in Jurevičius et al. [31] and NaviLoc [32], is the planned fix.

07How we checked the results

  • Paired statistics. Every comparison uses the same frames and an exact McNemar test [33], reported per region and pooled. Claims that did not pass were not made.
  • Grid-placement control. We rebuilt the r05 map with identical tiles but the grid shifted by 50 m. Accuracy moved from 74.3% to 62.5%. Where the tile centres fall relative to the flight line is worth up to ±12 points, a known effect of map sampling [37]. Since then, maps of different tile size are compared only with their tile centres aligned, and one earlier claim (exact rotation beats 90° steps) was withdrawn.
  • A data bug found and fixed. The r05 map uses a 100 m tile spacing, not the 125 m its name suggests. Maps built at the wrong spacing were deleted and rebuilt.
  • Independent cross-checks. The camera constant was measured with two unrelated encoders, and on frames outside the evaluation set.
  • No test labels in the method. We did not copy one published method [38] because its alignment step is fitted on ground-truth test pairs.
  • Reproducible. Every number comes from saved JSON/CSV outputs. The shipped command reproduces the experiment within 1–2 frames per region, and the new code has 14 unit tests.

10Next steps

  1. Tighter evaluation. Denser tile spacing (50 m) to remove grid luck, and all 11 UAV-VisLoc regions instead of 4.
  2. Ship the best pipeline (MegaLoc + heading + flight centering + gated altitude crop) as the default in the localizer and the GUI.
  3. Learned matcher for re-ranking (LightGlue, GIM, MatchAnything, XFeat) for metre-level positions and a trustworthy confidence.
  4. Robust fusion: a particle filter or trajectory consensus in front of the Kalman filter, tested on sequences with a real IMU.
  5. More encoders, then compress: CAMP and DINOv3-SAT; then distill the ensemble into one small model for the Jetson.
  6. Hardware: buy an Orin Nano 8 GB Super, export to TensorRT and measure.
  7. Our own flights over the target area with logged camera, IMU, barometer and RTK ground truth.
